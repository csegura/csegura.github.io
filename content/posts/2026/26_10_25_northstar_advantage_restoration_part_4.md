## Making a case for the drive bays

The NorthStar Advantage has two drive bays, and I want to use them with Gotek floppy-drive emulators. A Gotek is a device that can stand in for a floppy disk drive. It uses a USB flash drive to provide disk images instead of physical floppy disks.

The original drives belong to a period when 5¼-inch floppy disks were common. Shugart Associates helped make this drive size popular in the 1970s. These drives mattered because they gave personal computers a practical way to load and save programs. The old drives in my Advantage are part of its history, but an emulator can make it easier to use without relying on aging disks and drive parts.

I am designing and printing cases to fit the Advantage’s drive bays. The pieces in this photo include flat panels, side walls, holes and slots. They have visible print layers, but look intact. One small piece has “IBM” printed on it. I cannot tell its purpose from the photo.

## The Gotek board and its controls

The main board is clearly marked “GOTEK System” and “SFRKC30.AT3.” Gotek floppy emulators became useful as original floppy drives and disks became harder to maintain. They offer a way to use disk images from USB storage while keeping a familiar drive-style connection to a vintage computer. The board shown here has a USB-A socket, rows of pins, small electronic parts, and red and green indicator lights. It also says “Made in China.”

I have wired the board inside a drive-sized enclosure, but the assembly is still open. A small blue board marked “OLED” is connected at the edge. OLED means organic light-emitting diode, a type of display in which the pixels make their own light. The screen itself is not visible in this view. The wires and exposed electronics show that this is a work in progress, rather than a finished, closed case.

![Open Gotek emulator board wired inside a black drive enclosure.](https://i.imgur.com/0r5uJd2.jpeg)

The Gotek’s original firmware is the software that controls how the device behaves. I am replacing the firmware as part of this project. Different firmware can change how an emulator handles disk images and controls, but I do not have the firmware name, version, or installation steps in these notes. The photo confirms the board type, but it does not show which firmware is installed or whether the replacement is complete.

## Testing in the Advantage

The host computer is a NorthStar Advantage 8/16. North Star Computers made the Advantage in the early 1980s. Its all-in-one design brought the computer, CRT screen, keyboard, and drive bays together in one machine. The computer’s two drive bays make this emulator project a useful way to keep the system practical while preserving its original form.

In the full view, both drive areas are open or modified. Small electronics, blue USB devices, and red lights can be seen. The case and keyboard look largely intact, though the new drive setup is visible. The screen shows a green graphics display, which confirms that the computer is running, but it does not by itself confirm that the Goteks are reading disk images.

![Vintage Advantage 8/16 computer with CRT screen and modified drive bays.](https://i.imgur.com/564Yjx9.jpeg)

The close-up of the CRT shows North Star Graphics Disk Operating System, Version 2.0.0. A CRT, or cathode-ray tube, creates an image by sending electrons to a coated screen. The monitor’s exact model is not visible. The text identifies the operating system on screen, but it does not show the emulator’s firmware or a disk-loading test.
